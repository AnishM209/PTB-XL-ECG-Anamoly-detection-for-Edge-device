1. The system in one page

The device records one ECG lead from two chest electrodes. The positive input (IN1P of an ADS1299 analogue front end) sits at the V2 position, just left of the sternum in the 4th intercostal space, and the negative input (SRB1) at the V1 position, just right of the sternum at the same height. A third electrode drives the bias. A PYNQ-Z2 board reads the ADC and sends the samples to a laptop over a serial link.

The ADC runs at 250 samples per second. The original plan was 500, but the board’s acquisition loop could only keep up with 250, so the ADC was set to 250 and the signal is upsampled to 500 Hz in software before the models.

Two neural networks make the decisions. Both are 1D ResNets (resnet1d_wang_s5, about 536,000 parameters each) trained on the public PTB-XL database, using the lead V2 minus V1, which is exactly what two electrodes at V1 and V2 measure:

  • Binary model: normal versus abnormal (any of myocardial infarction, ST/T change, conduction disturbance, hypertrophy). It looks at 2.5 s windows.
  • AF model: atrial fibrillation or flutter versus not. It looks at 5 s windows.

Every 10 s of signal is cut into overlapping windows (50 % overlap), each window is scored, and the scores are averaged. A score above a threshold means “Abnormal” or “AF”. The thresholds were chosen on PTB-XL fold 9 (the validation fold); all reported results are on fold 10 (the test fold, 2,158 records for the binary task, 2,098 for AF).

The question this document answers: which filtering should sit between the ADC and the models? The models were trained on PTB-XL signals as they come, so any filter we add changes what the models see. A filter helps only if it removes interference the models would otherwise be confused by, without changing the heartbeat shapes they rely on.

2. Terms used in this document

  • Mains interference: the 50 Hz hum from the electricity supply that the body picks up like an antenna.
  • Notch filter: a filter that removes one narrow frequency band (here 50 Hz) and passes everything else.
  • Q (quality factor): centre frequency divided by the width of the notch at −3 dB. A high Q is a narrow notch, a low Q a wide one. At 50 Hz, Q = 30 is about 1.7 Hz wide and Q = 5 about 10 Hz wide (45 to 55 Hz).
  • IIR filter: an infinite impulse response filter, a recursive filter that uses past outputs as well as past inputs. IIR filters are cheap (a handful of multiplications per sample) and suit a microcontroller or FPGA. The notch and the Butterworth filters here are IIR.
  • Causal: the filter only uses past and present samples, as it must in real time. Causal IIR filters shift different frequencies by different amounts of time (phase distortion), which matters for the ECG’s shape.
  • Zero-phase: filtering forward and backward (filtfilt) so that there is no phase distortion. Only possible offline, on a recorded signal.
  • Baseline wander: slow drift of the signal (breathing, electrode movement), mostly below 0.5 Hz.
  • SWT (stationary wavelet transform): splits a signal into frequency bands (detail bands D1 to D7 and an approximation A7) without downsampling, so every band stays aligned sample by sample with the original.
  • AUROC: area under the ROC curve, a threshold-free measure of how well a model separates the two classes (1.0 perfect, 0.5 chance). ΔAUROC is the change against a reference. We report it with a 95 % paired bootstrap confidence interval; an asterisk (*) means the interval excludes zero, so the change is statistically significant.
  • Sensitivity / specificity: the fraction of positive records correctly flagged, and the fraction of negative records correctly passed, at the chosen threshold.
  • NSTDB: the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database, real noise recorded from Holter electrodes: baseline wander (bw), electrode motion (em) and muscle noise (ma). We add it to clean PTB-XL records at a chosen signal-to-noise ratio (SNR, in dB).

3. Why a notch filter is needed at all

PTB-XL recordings contain almost no mains interference. Our recordings do, and a lot of it. The PYNQ acquisition script measures the 50 Hz amplitude of every recording:

Recording50 Hz amplitudeNotes
First recordings (laptop on charger)16.8 mVabout 30 times the ECG
still_01 B (setup improved)0.28 mVbattery power, bias contact, short leads
still_01 C (reference recording)1.0 mVused for all later checks
move_01 (arm swings, twists, walking)1.4 rising to 42 mVelectrode contact degraded during movement

The ECG itself is about 1 mV peak to peak. Without a notch, mains is as large as or larger than the heartbeat. The real mains frequency measured in our recordings was between 49.97 and 50.02 Hz.

The notch is the one block that every working pipeline needs. In the test where the team pipeline ran without a notch on recording C, the binary model called 9 of 10 windows of a healthy, still subject “Abnormal”, because the team pipeline’s 150 Hz low-pass lets 50 Hz straight through.

The notch is a second-order IIR filter with two zeros on the unit circle at ±50 Hz and two poles just inside it at the same angle:

H(z) = (1 − 2cos(ω0) z−1 + z−2) / (1 − 2r cos(ω0) z−1 + r2 z−2),   ω0 = 2π · 50 / 250

The zeros force the gain to zero exactly at 50 Hz; the pole radius r (just below 1) sets how quickly the gain recovers on either side, and therefore Q. A wide notch (Q = 5) is more forgiving when the mains frequency drifts slightly or the notch is not exactly on it, and its impulse response dies out faster, so it rings less after a glitch. The price is that it also touches frequencies near 50 Hz and shifts the phase of the ECG’s faster components a little more.

4. Pipeline A: notch and low-pass, then the team pipeline

The team’s DSP pipeline (“First Phase”, following Berwal et al., IEEE Sensors Journal 2019) is a motion-artefact remover. It estimates the part of the signal that looks like artefact and subtracts it. Our teammate implemented it in Python, including a bank-by-bank version that mirrors the planned FPGA/processor split (bank_pipeline.py). We used the teammate’s current settings: 0.6 s median window, A7 band thresholded (not removed). In the tests this pipeline is called TC: notch, then low-pass 40 Hz, then the team pipeline.

ADC / resamplingMains notchLow-pass / band-passTeam pipeline blockModelsOutputs

What each block contributes

Notch 50 Hz, Q = 5 (IIR, causal)

Removes mains interference before anything else sees it. This placement matters for the team pipeline in particular: mains left in the signal lands in the SWT detail bands (D3 covers about 31 to 62 Hz), where it would distort the per-band thresholds and the R-peak detector. Without it the pipeline fails on real recordings (9 of 10 healthy windows Abnormal).

Low-pass 40 Hz, Butterworth order 2 (IIR, causal)

Removes muscle noise and any remaining high-frequency interference above the main ECG band. Gain is −3 dB at 40 Hz and falls by 12 dB per octave above it (−18 dB at 80 Hz). In our noise tests this block is what helped under muscle noise.

Upsample ×2 to 500 Hz (polyphase FIR)

The team pipeline and the models were designed for 500 Hz. resample_poly inserts samples and removes the spectral images with a windowed FIR filter, so the 250 Hz signal’s content is kept unchanged and nothing new is added above 125 Hz.

Median filter, 0.6 s (team block)

Estimates the baseline as the running median over 0.6 s and subtracts it, removing baseline wander. A median follows slow drift but ignores short events like the QRS complex. The window length is critical: the original 0.3 s window was short enough to follow the T wave, so it subtracted about 45 % of every T wave. At 0.6 s, 89 % of the T wave is kept (median over the test fold).

Low-pass 150 Hz (team block)

The low-pass from the original design, for high-frequency noise. After the 40 Hz low-pass in front it has little left to do, and it does not affect 50 Hz.

Stationary wavelet transform, Haar, 7 levels

Splits the signal into bands that are aligned in time with the original. At 500 Hz the bands are roughly: D1 125 to 250 Hz, D2 62 to 125, D3 31 to 62, D4 16 to 31, D5 8 to 16, D6 4 to 8, D7 2 to 4, A7 0 to 2 Hz. The QRS complex is strongest in D4; motion artefact is mostly in the lower bands.

Christov R-peak detector on D4

Finds the heartbeats using combined adaptive thresholds (slope, beat-amplitude and rhythm terms). Its beat positions are used for the QRS removal and, in the live demo, for the heart rate. We found and fixed a bug where a large start-up transient made the detector stop finding beats permanently (the reset patch in test_cleaning_reset).

QRS removal on D1 to D6

Sets the coefficients around each detected beat to zero (about ±50 ms plus the band’s filter length, up to ±178 ms in D6), so that the heartbeat itself is not mistaken for artefact in the next steps.

Threshold calculation and inverted thresholding

For every band, the per-second maximum and minimum over the last 16 s give an upper and a lower limit (median plus or minus the median absolute deviation). Coefficients inside the limits count as normal signal and are set to zero; coefficients outside them are kept as “artefact”. That is why it is called inverted: the output of this step is the artefact, not the ECG.

Inverse SWT and subtraction

The inverse SWT turns the kept artefact coefficients back into a time signal, the artefact estimate, which is subtracted from the preprocessed signal (after median and 150 Hz low-pass). The result is the cleaned ECG fed to the models. On the device, processing runs in 2,048-sample banks with a delay of 160 samples (0.32 s).

Where the notch sits, and could it go elsewhere?

In pipeline A the notch is in front of the whole team pipeline, where the PYNQ script already filters (the processor side), leaving the team’s blocks unchanged as one unit. It could also go right after the median filter (median, then notch and low-pass, then 150 Hz low-pass and SWT). The linear blocks commute, and a 0.6 s median of a signal with a 50 Hz sine on it still finds roughly the right baseline, so the result should be almost the same. What matters is that the notch comes before the SWT and the R-peak detector. We wrote a test version of the live demo with the notch after the median (live_demo_mn.py), but its thresholds have not been recalibrated, so it is not validated.

5. Pipeline B: notch Q5 and 40 Hz low-pass (device chain D3)

Pipeline B keeps only what the tests showed to be useful: a notch, a low-pass and a DC removal that cannot distort the heartbeat shape. It is the default model input of the live demo and the configuration we validated most thoroughly. In the tests it is called D3 (at 500 Hz in the chain test) or D (in the 250 Hz device setting).

ADC / resamplingMains notchLow-pass / band-passTeam pipeline blockModelsOutputs

What each block contributes

Notch 50 Hz, Q = 5 (IIR, causal)

The same notch as in pipeline A. Q = 5 was chosen over Q = 30 (the PYNQ script’s original value) because of the mains stress test (section 6.7): with Q = 30, as little as 1 mV of mains already halved the AF model’s sensitivity, while Q = 5 stayed close to clean performance up to 2 mV. Q = 5 costs a little on perfectly clean data (binary AUROC −0.0076), which is a good trade for a device whose mains level varied from 0.3 to 42 mV.

Low-pass 40 Hz, Butterworth order 2 (IIR, causal)

Removes muscle noise. Under NSTDB muscle noise it improved the binary model by about 0.007 to 0.010 AUROC and the AF model by up to 0.032, significant in each case. It costs nothing measurable on clean data.

Upsample ×2 to 500 Hz

As in pipeline A. The device-chain validation (section 6.6) showed that going down to 250 Hz and back up costs nothing measurable.

Window mean removal and standardisation

Each model window has its own mean subtracted, then the training statistics are applied (subtract mean μ ≈ −0.001 mV, divide by σ ≈ 0.255 mV). The mean removal deals with the electrode’s DC offset, which can be tens of mV. It replaces a high-pass filter on purpose: a causal 0.5 Hz high-pass created an undershoot after the T wave and tilted the ST segment, costing the binary model 0.014 to 0.016 AUROC. Subtracting a constant per window cannot change the beat shape.

Models and thresholds

Binary model on seven 2.5 s windows per 10 s, AF model on three 5 s windows, sigmoid outputs averaged. Thresholds re-chosen on PTB-XL fold 9 passed through this exact chain: binary 0.5750, AF 0.3286 (config_device_q5.json). Using the thresholds of a different chain would shift the operating point, because every chain shifts the models’ outputs slightly.

Display path and quality gate (live demo only)

For the screen, a second copy of the signal goes through the notch and a 0.5 to 40 Hz band-pass, which gives a flat, readable trace for people. The heart rate comes from peaks on this trace (or from the team’s Christov detector when the team pipeline is selected). A quality gate checks the raw signal and withholds the verdict if the 50 Hz amplitude is above 3 mV, the slow drift (below 0.5 Hz) above 0.3 mV rms, the 0.5 to 5 Hz content above 0.4 mV rms, the 5 to 40 Hz content above 0.3 mV rms, or fewer than two beats are found. These limits come from our still and movement recordings and are first estimates.

6. The tests, in the order we ran them

All PTB-XL tests use the V2 minus V1 lead, the fold-10 test records and the same seed-0 models. Each script first checks that it reproduces the known results on raw data (a self-check); every result below passed its self-check.

The first version of the team code (0.3 s median, A7 band removed completely) was run on the whole test fold.

The revised pipeline’s lower specificity at the original threshold disappeared after re-choosing the threshold on cleaned fold 9: sensitivity 0.73 and specificity 0.82, against 0.70 and 0.86 on raw.

Conclusion: with the revised settings the team pipeline is harmless on clean data, but it does not improve anything either.

The test also showed the practical risk of noise: the models’ thresholds drift. With electrode motion at 12 dB, the binary model’s specificity on raw input fell from 0.86 to 0.47, and electrode motion created false AF alarms while muscle noise hid real AF.

6.3 The PYNQ script’s own filters

The acquisition script originally filtered with a causal notch (Q = 30) and a 0.5 to 40 Hz band-pass. Split into parts on clean PTB-XL (binary ΔAUROC against raw): notch alone −0.002 (not significant), notch and 0.5 Hz high-pass −0.014*, notch and 40 Hz low-pass −0.003*, full band-pass −0.016*. The causal 0.5 Hz high-pass is the costly part: it undershoots after the T wave, tilts the ST segment, shrinks the T wave by about 15 % and delays the QRS by 5 to 10 ms. The 40 Hz low-pass helped under muscle noise (binary +0.007 to +0.010*, AF +0.013* at 12 dB, +0.032* at 6 dB).

Recordings from one healthy subject at rest showed a clean ECG (heart rate 64 to 70 bpm), the 250 samples per second limit of the board, mains of about 17 mV, and occasional short timing gaps (up to about 20 ms) that cause brief filter transients.

6.9 Real recordings with pipeline B

  • Still recording C: all ten windows Normal (binary probability 0.09 to 0.35 against threshold 0.575), no AF (at most 0.002), heart rate 63 to 71 bpm. With Q = 30 the results were nearly identical at this mains level (mean difference 0.03).
  • First recording, 17 mV mains: Q = 30 gave one false Abnormal and scores up to 0.50 higher than Q = 5, which first showed the effect later measured in the mains stress test.
  • Electrode polarity: flipping the sign of a normal recording made every window Abnormal (0.93 to 0.98), while AF was unaffected. The binary model depends on the lead orientation; swapped leads look like an abnormal heart. Recording B turned out to have swapped leads, which we saw from its mirror-image beat shape.
  • Movement (move_01): every window Abnormal and false AF during walking, as predicted by the electrode-motion results of the noise test. This is why the live demo has a quality gate.

7. Conclusions and decisions

In one sentence: the notch is essential, the 40 Hz low-pass helps under muscle noise, the per-window mean safely replaces a high-pass, and the team’s artefact-removal pipeline works as designed but does not make the models more accurate or more robust.

  1. A 50 Hz notch is mandatory on the device, in every pipeline, and it must come before the SWT and the R-peak detector if the team pipeline is used.
  2. Use Q = 5, not Q = 30. It costs 0.0076 binary AUROC on clean data and is far more robust to residual mains, which our device has. Keep mains at the source below 1 mV (battery power, good bias contact, short leads, subject isolated from earth).
  3. No causal high-pass in the model path. Remove the DC offset per window instead. The 0.5 to 40 Hz band-pass is fine for the display only.
  4. Pipeline B (D3) is the validated model input and the live demo’s default.
  5. Pipeline A (TC) also works on still signals and can be selected in the live demo (--pipeline team), at a small cost on PTB-XL (binary −0.0098 against −0.0076). It is useful when the presentation should show the team’s DSP pipeline producing the model input.
  6. Keep the team’s Christov detector for heart rate; it was the most reliable beat detector under moderate movement.
  7. Why the team pipeline does not help the models: the models were trained on unfiltered PTB-XL, so any cleaning shifts their input; the artefact estimate is subtracted from the ECG itself, so its errors end up in the beat shape; and it does not address mains, the dominant interference in practice. Karan’s independent report found the same for a band-pass in front of an unchanged model.
  8. The device works at 250 samples per second with upsampling, at no measurable cost.
  9. Lead orientation must be fixed (+ at V2, − at V1) and labelled; the live demo warns when the T wave is inverted on most beats.

8. Limitations and open questions

  • Real recordings come from one healthy subject: four still recordings and one movement recording. They show plausibility, not clinical validity, and they cannot show missed abnormalities.
  • NSTDB noise is added on top of clean signals; real movement also changes electrode contact and can alter the ECG itself. The noise comes from Holter electrodes, not our setup.
  • The synthetic mains model assumes up to ±0.05 Hz frequency offset, slightly more than we measured; Q = 30 is the setting most sensitive to this.
  • The quality-gate limits are first estimates from our own recordings.
  • Open: whether the team pipeline helps once the models are retrained on cleaned data; whether TB’s better behaviour on move_01 holds in a labelled noise test; whether moving the notch after the median filter changes anything (expected not; thresholds not yet computed).
  • Better robustness to movement most likely needs retraining with noise and mains augmentation on the device chain’s input, plus validation with labelled signals played through the real hardware.
